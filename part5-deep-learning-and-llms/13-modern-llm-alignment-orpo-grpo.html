Modern LLM Post-Training — DPO, ORPO, KTO, GRPO, RLAIF, and PPO

Where this fits Chapter 12 covered SFT (supervised fine‑tuning) and PEFT (LoRA / QLoRA) — how to make a base LLM follow your instructions and your domain. This chapter is about the next step: preference and reward-based post-training — how to improve choices after SFT, and how to decide whether an offline preference objective or an online RL loop matches the evidence you actually have.
Alignment reading path Chapter 12 covers the adaptation stack: SFT, PEFT, LoRA/QLoRA, evaluation, and an introduction to DPO/RLHF. This chapter intentionally repeats the DPO/RLHF baseline only so ORPO, KTO, GRPO, RLAIF, and PPO-style RLHF can be compared in one coherent decision section.

1) Build the map before comparing acronyms

Post-training discussions become confusing when three different decisions are collapsed into one:

DecisionQuestionExamples
Signal source Who or what says an output is good? Human comparison, thumbs-up/down, AI judge, unit test, schema validator.
Data shape What evidence reaches the trainer? Demonstration, chosen/rejected pair, binary label, prompt plus numeric reward.
Optimization method How are model parameters changed? SFT, DPO, ORPO, KTO, GRPO, PPO.

RLAIF, for example, describes feedback produced by an AI system. That feedback can train a reward model for PPO, create pairs for DPO or ORPO, or create binary labels for KTO. RLAIF is therefore a signal source and data-generation process, not a competing optimizer.

1.1 The classic RLHF lineage

RLHF (Christiano et al., 2017; InstructGPT, 2022) commonly collects human preference pairs, fits a reward model, and then uses PPO to optimize an SFT policy against that learned reward. A KL term or related constraint limits destructive drift from a reference policy.

At the algorithm level, PPO-style RLHF has four distinct roles: policy, reference or old-policy distribution, reward model or grader, and value/critic estimate. That does not mean four complete models must always be resident in VRAM. Backbones can be shared, adapters can separate roles, reference scores can be precomputed, and components can be sharded or offloaded. The roles explain the algorithm; a profiler explains the memory bill.

1.2 Why direct preference methods appeared

DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023) showed that, under its preference model assumptions, a policy can be optimized directly from chosen/rejected pairs without fitting an explicit reward model and without an online PPO rollout loop. KTO and ORPO change the required data or reference-policy role; GRPO returns to online sampling but removes the learned critic. These methods therefore trade different forms of complexity rather than forming a single strict ladder from old to new.

2) DPO recap (the baseline)

DPO assumes a Bradley–Terry preference model and trains directly on triples $(x, y_w, y_l)$ where $y_w$ is the chosen response and $y_l$ is the rejected one. The loss is:

$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}} = -\log\sigma\!\left( \beta \log\dfrac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_w|x)} - \beta \log\dfrac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_l|x)} \right)$

The objective compares the trainable policy with a fixed reference policy. The scale $\beta$ controls the strength of that reference-relative regularization; in the common interpretation, larger values penalize departure more strongly. A strong SFT checkpoint is the usual practical starting point, and the method requires paired preferences whose chosen and rejected responses answer the same prompt.

Operationally: DPO needs reference log-probabilities, but not necessarily a second fully resident model throughout training. Current implementations can keep a frozen reference model, precompute its log-probabilities, or derive policy and reference behavior from different adapters over one base checkpoint.

  • Advantage: no learned reward model and no on-policy rollout loop.
  • Risk: contradictory, position-biased, or trivially easy pairs teach the wrong preference boundary.
  • Use when: paired preferences are trustworthy and offline training is sufficient.

3) ORPO — Odds‑Ratio Preference Optimization (Hong et al., 2024)

ORPO removes the reference model entirely and merges SFT and preference optimization into a single loss:

$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ORPO}} = \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}}(y_w \mid x) + \lambda \cdot \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{OR}}$

where $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{OR}}$ is the negative log‑sigmoid of the log odds‑ratio between the chosen and rejected responses:

$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{OR}} = -\log\sigma\!\left(\log\dfrac{\mathrm{odds}_\theta(y_w|x)}{\mathrm{odds}_\theta(y_l|x)}\right), \quad \mathrm{odds}_\theta(y|x) = \dfrac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{1-\pi_\theta(y|x)}$

In implementation, those probabilities are computed from sequence log probabilities or length-normalized token log probabilities. The displayed formula is the intuition; raw full-sequence probabilities are tiny, so real code works in log space for numerical stability.

Why people like it:

  • No frozen reference policy is required, so one major source of memory and compute overhead is removed. The actual saving depends on adapters, precomputation, sharding, optimizer state, and sequence length; there is no universal “50 percent” guarantee.
  • You can go from a base model directly to an aligned model in a single ORPO training objective rather than running separate SFT and preference phases. Starting-model and data quality still matter.
  • The ORPO paper reported competitive results across Phi-2, Llama-2, and Mistral experiments up to 7B parameters; that is evidence from those settings, not a guarantee for every domain.

Use when: you have paired preference data and want the simplest, reference-free candidate to benchmark. In current TRL, ORPOTrainer is experimental and has its own configuration and constructor contract; do not assume it is a drop-in alias for DPOTrainer.

4) KTO — Kahneman–Tversky Optimization (Ethayarajh et al., 2024)

KTO removes the requirement for paired preference data. Instead of $(x, y_w, y_l)$ triples, you label each $(x, y)$ as simply desirable or undesirable — the kind of data you can collect at scale from thumbs‑up / thumbs‑down telemetry.

The loss is shaped after Kahneman–Tversky prospect theory: gains on desirable samples and losses on undesirable samples are weighted asymmetrically (loss aversion). This makes KTO a natural candidate for product telemetry where users rarely write paired comparisons but can still provide a positive or negative signal. It does not make raw clicks automatically valid labels: selection bias, accidental clicks, exposure bias, and class imbalance still need explicit treatment.

Use when: your feedback is binary, unpaired, and noisy (production telemetry, customer ratings). Like DPO, the standard KTO formulation uses a reference-policy baseline; current TRL can precompute those reference log-probabilities.

5) The crucial split: offline preference learning vs online RL

DPO, ORPO, and KTO are normally offline: the training set already contains responses and preference information. PPO and GRPO are online policy optimization: the current policy generates new responses during training, a reward function scores them, and those fresh samples drive the update.

ModeMain benefitMain risk
Offline preference optimization Reproducible data, simpler infrastructure, no rollout serving loop. The fixed dataset may not cover behavior produced by the changing policy.
Online RL Training explores the policy's current outputs and can optimize a direct grader. Rollouts are expensive, and a flawed reward can be exploited in new ways.

This distinction is more useful than saying that one acronym is universally “more modern.” Choose the loop that matches your feedback and evaluation system.

6) GRPO — Group Relative Policy Optimization (DeepSeekMath and DeepSeek‑R1)

GRPO was introduced in the DeepSeekMath work and later used as an important RL component in the DeepSeek‑R1 program. It is closer to PPO than to DPO in spirit: the current policy samples responses and learns from rewards. Its key simplification is that it removes the learned value/critic network and uses relative rewards within a group of responses instead.

For each prompt $x$ you sample a group of $G$ responses $\{y_1, \dots, y_G\}$ from the current policy. Each is scored by a reward function $r_i$ (which can be a learned reward model, but in practice is often a verifiable signal: unit‑test pass/fail, math answer match, format check). Compute the advantage as the in‑group standardization:

$A_i = \dfrac{r_i - \mathrm{mean}(r_1,\dots,r_G)} {\mathrm{std}(r_1,\dots,r_G) + \varepsilon}$

The small $\varepsilon$ in this teaching form prevents division by zero. If every response in a group receives the same reward, the group provides no relative learning signal and should be logged rather than silently treated as informative.

The original family of objectives applies a policy-gradient or clipped update using the group-relative advantage and can include a KL penalty against a reference policy. Removing the critic reduces one substantial model role and its training burden. It does not guarantee lower total memory or better stability in every system: sampling $G$ completions per prompt is expensive, and sequence length, rollout concurrency, inference acceleration, and optional reference-policy use can dominate the footprint.

Why it matters:

  • Uses verifiable rewards (RLVR). For math and code you don't need a reward model at all — graders are deterministic.
  • The group mean acts as a Monte-Carlo baseline, replacing the learned value network with comparisons among sibling responses.
  • The DeepSeek‑R1 report describes GRPO-style RL with verifiable and model-based rewards as one major ingredient in its reasoning gains.

Use when: your task has a programmatic grader (math, code, JSON‑schema compliance, tool‑use success) — i.e. RLVR settings.

Do not reduce DeepSeek‑R1 to one optimizer DeepSeek‑R1 was a multi-stage training pipeline: cold-start supervised data, reasoning-oriented RL, rejection sampling and additional SFT, followed by further alignment RL. DeepSeek‑R1‑Zero was the experiment that began with large-scale RL without preliminary SFT. “GRPO trained R1” is therefore true only as a partial description.

6.1 Current implementation caveats

GRPO implementations have continued to evolve. Current TRL documentation defaults to no KL reference model when beta=0 and exposes several loss-normalization choices because the original sequence-level formulation can introduce response-length bias. Group standard-deviation scaling can also create question-difficulty bias. Treat reward scaling, zero-variance groups, completion truncation, and length normalization as first-class evaluation variables, not harmless defaults.

7) RLAIF and Constitutional AI are related, not synonyms

RLAIF (reinforcement learning from AI feedback) uses an AI system to produce some or all of the preference signal that human annotators would otherwise provide. A capable judge can compare response pairs against a rubric, assign scalar scores, or label desirable and undesirable outputs. Those labels may then feed a reward model, DPO, ORPO, KTO, PPO, or another optimizer.

Constitutional AI is a more specific procedure. In the original work, a written constitution guides self-critique and revision during a supervised phase, then AI-generated preference labels support an RL phase. A normal RLAIF judge does not automatically become Constitutional AI merely because its prompt contains a rubric.

AI feedback can scale beyond what human labelers can review directly, but the judge introduces its own position bias, verbosity bias, self-preference, rubric ambiguity, and correlated blind spots. Calibrate it against a human-reviewed holdout set, randomize answer order, allow ties or abstention, and keep judge and policy versions in the experiment record. Active learning can reserve expensive judgments for uncertain or high-impact cases, but the uncertainty rule itself must be evaluated.

9) A practical enterprise decision sequence

  1. Define the held-out evaluation suite, safety constraints, and acceptable regressions before changing the model.
  2. Establish a strong SFT baseline on domain demonstrations (LoRA / QLoRA where appropriate — Chapter 12).
  3. Collect provenance-rich feedback. Thumbs-up/down can seed KTO, while reviewed chosen/rejected pairs can seed DPO or ORPO. Do not convert ambiguous telemetry into labels without a sampling and audit policy.
  4. Benchmark at least one simple offline baseline. DPO is a strong default; KTO fits unpaired labels; ORPO remains a useful reference-free candidate. Choose from evaluation evidence, not an assumed VRAM percentage.
  5. For extraction, JSON, code, SQL, math, or tool-use tasks with reliable graders, compare rejection-sampling/SFT baselines with a small GRPO or other online-RL experiment. A narrow grader may be fewer than 100 lines of Python, but adversarial tests, sandboxing, observability, and governance are usually the larger job.
  6. Use RLAIF when a written rubric and calibrated judge can expand coverage. DPO, KTO, ORPO, reward-model training, and PPO remain alternative consumers of that feedback; “RLAIF → ORPO” is one candidate, not a universal recipe.
  7. Re-measure the committed product metrics and the full regression suite. Ship through a canary only when the intended gain survives human review and safety evaluation.
Common pitfall Alignment methods reward what you measure. If your reward model — or your verifiable grader — has a loophole, the policy will find it (reward hacking). Always hold out an evaluation set and inspect samples by hand every iteration.

10) Failure modes to test explicitly

  • Reward hacking: the policy satisfies the grader while violating the real intent.
  • Preference inconsistency: annotators or judges apply different criteria to similar pairs.
  • Judge bias: order, length, style, or model-family cues determine the AI judge's choice.
  • Distribution shift: offline pairs do not resemble outputs from the updated policy or production traffic.
  • Zero-variance groups: every GRPO completion gets the same reward, so the prompt contributes no relative signal.
  • Length and truncation bias: objective normalization or a maximum completion length changes what behavior is rewarded.
  • Capability regression: target metrics improve while general instruction following, calibration, or safety deteriorates.
